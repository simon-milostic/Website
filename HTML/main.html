DEBT SUSTAINABILITY

This article explains debt sustainability and how governments can manage their debt without risking default or needing exceptional financial assistance. Borrowing can help fund important projects and support economic development, but it is important to weigh the benefits against the cost of repaying the debt. A country's ability to manage its debt depends on factors such as economic growth, interest rates, the quality of its institutions, and how effectively it manages public finances. The article also highlights the importance of reliable debt data and careful planning to reduce the risk of debt becoming unsustainable.

WHAT IS DEBT?

The following article explains what debt is, how it arises and the main concepts within the United States. Along with the summary, there is a link leading to the original page. In a given fiscal year, government’s spending can exceed its revenue, creating a deficit. To cover this deficit, government indulges in selling bonds, bills, notes, TIPS and more. Although usually seen as negative, national debt allows governments to pay for important programs even when the funds from federal revenues aren’t available. It is smart to compare debt-to-GDP ratio as it shows us the approximate ability for a government to repay its debt. The national debt can be broken down by whether it is non-marketable or marketable and whether it is debt held by the public or debt held by the government itself. Finally, the debt ceiling sets the legal limit on how much debt the federal government can have at a given time.

INTEREST BURDEN

An official United Nations report examining the issue of debt in developing countries highlights a positive-feedback loop between financing future investments and servicing existing debt. The analysis builds up to an alarming fact: approximately 3.3 billion people live in countries that spend more on interest payments than on education or healthcare. Read more at Arise News.

RICH-WORLD DEBT

Rich countries borrow in their own currencies and have deep bond markets, so they rarely face the kind of crisis developing countries do. But that doesn’t make their debt limitless. Debt becomes unsustainable when interest costs grow faster than the economy and governments can’t run big enough surpluses to stop it. Markets have started noticing: the IMF points to recent bond-market repricing in Japan, the United States, and parts of Europe as investors question whether large economies can borrow without limit.
